Overview

Our second hackathon put about 80 people in one room at the CUNY Graduate Center. June ran on an open brief. September gave teams two scoped themes: tools that help New York's 59 community boards run and tools that help the city capture, triage and act on what New Yorkers say.

Teams formed at 5:30 around color-coded name tags. Each team of four paired someone who could build with someone who knew the domain and someone on data or design. Every team confirmed its idea with a judge before building. Build time ran from 6:30 to 8:30 and eight experts from city and state agencies, Civic Roundtable and Block Party checked in on the hour. Demos were three minutes a team: one minute of demo and one minute of judge questions. No slides. The NYC Open Data team showed the room how a city dataset becomes open data and where to ask for one that is not yet public.

The strongest projects came from people who knew the problem firsthand: board members who read a dozen PDFs before every meeting, residents who had sat through rezoning hearings and organizers who watch complaints pile up on social media with no path to an agency. The winner and two runners-up were invited to show their tools at the CUNY PIT Lab pop-up at the Oculus.

Co-hosted with Civic Roundtable and CUNY PIT Lab. Civic Roundtable covered dinner.

The goal

What we set out to do:

  • Build something that makes being heard actually work. June proved a room can ship 20 prototypes in an evening. September tested whether tighter problem statements produce tools closer to adoption.
  • Put the judges' own problems on the table. Each judge named one build they wanted to see. Teams matched real needs instead of guessing at them.
  • Keep the people who can adopt a tool in the room. Experts from NYC OTI, NYC HPD and the state's customer experience office told teams whether a prototype matched how the work runs.
What the judges looked for

Judges scored every demo on five questions. The second one is specific to this theme.

  • Does it address a real operational constraint?
  • Whose voice does it bring in that today's process leaves out?
  • Does the data exist to support it?
  • Would practitioners understand how to use it?
  • Is there a pathway to real deployment?
Projects produced

The winner, the two runners-up and three more tools from the night. Every tool with a public link is in the Proof Points gallery.

Winner
Budget Requests
Every community board files Capital and Expense Budget Requests under the City Charter. Until now the requests and the agency responses sat scattered across NYC Open Data and the Register PDFs. Budget Requests puts them in one place for every board since 2019. Click a district on the map to read its requests and how each agency answered. A planning view sets the district's 311 complaint categories against its past requests to show the gaps. The app also drafts the follow-up email to the agency or elected official.

Runner-up · Hosted on this site
Hearts & Minds
Agencies rarely have the budget for user research and vendors build from requirements instead. Hearts & Minds gives the NYS Office of Customer Experience a radar for Medicaid: the biggest problems New Yorkers hit, laid out in the order people move through the program. Box size is people affected and shade is how often the problem comes up online. Select a problem to see its 90-day trend and public YouTube stories told in residents' own words. Runner-up
In the Zone
Rezoning fights stall when residents and planners argue from different information. In the Zone skips modeling the full land use process and draws one map: where density is low near transit and what would change if those lots allowed housing. Both sides start from the same picture.

Themes

Two defined problem areas and a third that opened once the judges spoke. Teams gathered by problem area. A team taking a Judge Special agreed the scope with that judge before build time started.

Community board operations
Judges: Raj Korpan, Sara Lind
New York's 59 community boards run on volunteer members and a staff of two or three. The theme offered three build paths: a meeting assistant for parliamentary procedure and resolution writing, a knowledge-base chatbot that speeds up onboarding before the 2027 term-limit turnover and a tracker that links each resolution to the right agency and follows the outcome.
Public voice at scale
Judges: Jeremy Ney, Amaury Dujardin, Abbey Kos
New Yorkers filed 3,655,040 requests through 311 in 2025. The theme asked three questions. What 311 complaint patterns can you find and whose voices are missing from the data? What could social media and other unstructured channels solve? How can agencies seek out and digest input from constituents they do not reach today?
Judge specials
One build each judge asked for
Each judge named one tool they wanted to see built. The asks are on the judge cards below. They ranged from upzoning candidates around Bronx transit hubs to stories told in people's own faces and voices.
Expert judges
Raj Korpan
Assistant Professor of Computer Science, Hunter College, CUNY; Associate Director for Research & Community Engagement, CUNY PIT Lab · Community board operations
Raj directs Hunter's Trustworthy, Intelligent, and Explainable Robotics (TIER) Lab. He works on explainable AI, human-robot interaction and inclusive technology design. He pushed teams on who their AI is designed for and whose perspectives are missing.
Asked teams to build: a tool that helps communities organize around local issues, use data to strengthen their case and engage their community board effectively.
Jeremy Ney
Author, American Inequality and The Opportunity Map · Public voice at scale
Jeremy maps America's regional divides county by county for more than 25,000 readers. TIME, The New York Times and NPR have covered the work. He works on tech policy at Google and teaches at Columbia. He pushed teams on what the data reveals that conventional wisdom misses and whether the insight improves lives for the least advantaged.
Asked teams to build: a map of the areas around transit hubs that could be upzoned for transit-oriented housing (C8, M1 or R3-5 zones), with a focus on the Bronx.
Sara Lind
Co-Executive Director, Open Plans · Community board operations
Sara leads Open Plans, a nonprofit changing how New Yorkers experience streets and public space. She has driven policy agendas in City Hall and Albany and shaped the public case for livable streets. She pushed teams on whether the tool gives New Yorkers a real role in shaping the spaces they share.
Asked teams to build: a tool that makes community boards accessible and accountable to the public between meetings and online as well as in the room.
Amaury Dujardin
Public Policy Manager, Citizens Union · Public voice at scale
Amaury leads Citizens Union's legislative advocacy on corruption, democracy and effective government in Albany and City Hall and manages its candidate endorsements. He spent nearly a decade in politics before moving to policy. He pushed teams on whether the tool closes the gap between constituents and the officials who represent them.
Asked teams to build: a tracker of the issues that come up most in public engagement, drawn from a sample that reflects a district's or the city's population.
Abbey Kos
Director of Customer Experience, State of New York · Public voice at scale
Governor Hochul appointed Abbey in March 2025. She has led digital transformations for federal, state and local government in the US and UK for more than 15 years, including three years at 18F and three at AWS. She is a user researcher and content designer by background. She pushed teams on operationalization: what would make officials want to work with you, how they would act on the information and how the design survives.
Asked teams to build: something that goes beyond text to include people's faces, voices and stories.
Experts in the room

Eight practitioners roamed the tables and ran check-ins at 7:00 and 8:00. Their job was to say where a prototype did not match how the work runs.

What came next

The top three teams take their tools to the Oculus. Budget Requests, Hearts & Minds and In the Zone were invited to the CUNY PIT Lab pop-up there.

Then a demo night. Teams that commit to keep building return to show their tools to the practitioners who can adopt them. SCE volunteers, experts and judges support them in between. The date goes out on the Slack and the Substack first. What to expect from a demo night →
